Agentic privacy, mapped

A long workshop report from the Google Contextual Agent Privacy and Security (CAPS) Workshop, New York City, November 2025, argues that the usual toolkit of least privilege, consent prompts and static filters breaks once software plans its own steps. Its replacement is an old idea from privacy philosophy: an agent should do what is appropriate in context. This map walks the framework, then every open problem the authors list, one area per page.

The one-sentence version: an agent needs broad access to be useful, and broad access is exactly what security engineering tells you not to grant, so the report proposes replacing "who may see what" with "which flows and actions fit the norms of this context". It calls that Contextual Integrity for information and Contextual Security for every other action, then spends most of its pages on what nobody yet knows how to build: norms a machine can look up, policies compiled from natural language, credentials that live exactly as long as a task, consent that does not fatigue, benchmarks that run for years, and governance that decides whose norms win.

01 · THE LENS

Why "appropriate in context" and not "allowed or denied"

The executive summary blames three properties of agents for breaking conventional security and privacy engineering. Part I then brings in Helen Nissenbaum's theory of Contextual Integrity as the yardstick for what "acting appropriately" means.

Dimension 1
Unstructured interfaces, ambiguous input

Instructions and data arrive as natural language and images, so expected behavior is hard to define and adversarial text (prompt injection) looks like any other input.

Dimension 2
Probabilistic, dynamic control flow

Plans and code are generated at run time with "jagged" model performance, so the execution path is new each time and resists ordinary testing.

Dimension 3
Autonomy and delegation

Long tasks, sub-agents and abstract goals outrun oversight that leans on confirmation prompts, which ends in consent fatigue.

Three dimensions, four threat models
Exec. summary, §1, §3capability vs. control
The three properties that break least privilege, read against the four threat models of §3: malicious users, a malicious environment (indirect prompt injection), a poisoned model, and no adversary at all, where agents still over-share, melt down and reward-hack. Plus the paper's reframing of prompt injection as a flip of judgment that can go either way.
02 · PART I · THE FRAMEWORK

From a theory of privacy to a machine that enforces it

Part I closes with two moves: extend Contextual Integrity from information flows to all behavior, and decompose "operationalize CI" into five steps an agent would have to perform. 03 · PART II · COMPONENTS

System, model, user

Part II asks what each layer can contribute: guardrails in the execution environment, contextual reasoning in the model, and oversight from a user whose attention is finite. Twenty-one open problems.

05
Identity, authorization, accounting
§7.1–7.3OP 7.1–7.5system level
An agent is neither a human nor a deterministic program, so identity, OAuth scopes and audit logs all wobble. Telling agents from people, tracing a sub-agent's action back to a user, purpose-bound and task-lived credentials, and artifacts that bind an action to the intent behind it.
06
PII, minimization, deletion, anonymity
§7.4OP 7.6–7.9system level
Redaction that keeps meaning, leaks through chain of thought and memory, three faces of data minimization, encrypted-traffic fingerprints, "ghost" representations that survive deletion, and the tension between anonymity and accountability.
07
AI as the last-mile developer
§7.5OP 7.10–7.11exec(*)
The report's sharpest systems argument: replace each LLM call with a nondeterministic choice and see what the code still guarantees. An email-to-calendar app stays bounded; a general planner becomes exec(*). How much agentic-ness a task needs, and how to bring testing and review back.
08
Teaching models contextual reasoning
§8OP 8.1–8.4model level
RL gyms and on-policy distillation for CI reasoning, the knowing-doing gap, when to ask a clarifying question, stale assumptions months later, scaling and verifier trade-offs, and interpretability for how models use private data.
09
Mental models and the end of notice-and-choice
§9.1–9.2OP 9.1–9.4user level
Why users misread agents, what merged contexts and "infinite memory" do to bystanders, four ways static consent fails, and a shift to risk-scaled, mixed-initiative control with policies users can author and edit.
10
Trust, stop buttons, accountability
§9.3OP 9.5–9.6user level
Trust versus trustworthiness, audit paths the task model cannot rewrite, "what if" simulation before delegation, delayed execution, undo windows and circuit breakers, and what contracts, transparency, liability and incentives mean when the contractor is a model.
04 · PART III · ECOSYSTEM

Evaluation, multi-agent systems, governance

Part III leaves the single agent: how to measure success, what happens when agents talk to agents, and who decides the norms. Twenty open problems.

11
Evaluations and benchmarks
§10, App. DOP 10.1–10.716 CI benchmarks reviewed
Why agent benchmarks mislead, the open question behind saturated red-teaming, the missing large-scale user studies, and "CI for life": memory over years, cumulative leakage, a privacy-utility balance that moves, and benchmarks that survive a change of harness.
12
Shared norms, delegation, composition
§11.1–11.2OP 11.1–11.5multi-agent
A contextual handshake between strangers, collusion and norm drift, constraints that evaporate after hop one, swarms of thousands, and disclosures that are each fine until someone sees both.
13
Protocols, attestation, side channels
§11.3–11.4OP 11.6–11.9multi-agent
Free-form language over MCP and A2A, firewalled task-scoped protocols, why remote attestation of code does not attest behavior, how expressive an inter-agent language should be, timing leaks, and simulated societies for training and evaluation.
14
Governance: whose norms, and who checks
§12–13OP 12.1–12.4norm repository
Preconditions for a norm-respecting ecosystem, liability placed where capability sits (the credit-card and ADA precedents), three ways to source norms, conflicting jurisdictions, and compliance records produced at the moment an agent acts.
